# Grade 8 — Personal Voice and Nationals Readiness: Weekly Plan

Fall 2026 | 16 weeks | Target: a reliable five-minute Original Oratory

This is a proposed teaching plan derived from the supplied Grade 8 curriculum and the Speak Feedback research playbook. It is not a record of historical lessons or assigned homework. Prompts and pacing can be adapted to the group while the phase outcomes remain visible. No dates or roster are assumed.

## How to use this plan

The sequence assumes a proposed adaptable 120-minute class: 10 minutes arrival and retrieval, 20 minutes explicit teaching/model, 15 minutes planning, 10 minutes break, 35 minutes coached attempts, 20 minutes feedback and retry, and 10 minutes exit record. Shorter or longer sessions can scale the practice blocks. Each cycle is teach → attempt → specific feedback → retry; record what was actually observed.

## Weeks 1–4 — Diagnostic and topic selection

### Week 1 — Baseline and the shape of an original perspective

- **Objective:** Establish a 3–5-minute speaking baseline and distinguish a topic, a personal perspective, and a message an audience can carry away.
- **Materials / prompt:** Timer, simple recording option with consent, one-page idea bank, and prompt: “What ordinary issue deserves a more interesting question?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach purpose/audience/message and the four-beat OO arc (opening, development, turn, close). Students give a low-stakes baseline from a familiar issue; listeners write the message they heard and one moment of uncertainty. Give one observable content or delivery cue, then repeat the opening and close.
- **Speaking output:** One 3–5-minute baseline plus a 30-second explanation of the speaker’s intended message and audience.
- **Proposed homework:** List five possible issues and, for each, write the intended audience, the question underneath it, and one personal point of access. Read the list aloud once; this is a proposal for practice, not a historical assignment.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can a listener state the speaker’s message? Record timing, strongest evidence of connection, and the single next repair without inferring confidence or personality.
- **Support / extension:** Offer a choice of familiar prompts, a keyword card, or audio-only delivery. Extend by asking for two different audiences for the same issue.

### Week 2 — Topic scan: breadth before commitment

- **Objective:** Evaluate breadth of possibilities before choosing, using personal access, analytical depth, audience relevance, and originality potential.
- **Materials / prompt:** Coach-curated scan of 10–20 topic areas, four-column evaluation grid, and sample topic-to-question transformations.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model turning a broad label into a contestable question. Students rapidly annotate 10–20 coach-selected topics, select three, and give 45-second mini-analyses. Peers identify the claim, missing context, and audience stake. Coach one revision; students repeat the strongest mini-analysis with clearer framing.
- **Speaking output:** Ten-to-twenty-topic scan and three spoken mini-analyses, each with a provisional perspective and one question for research.
- **Proposed homework:** Narrow three candidates to two; write a one-sentence perspective, one likely audience, and two questions for each. Rehearse both explanations aloud once.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Do the candidates contain a real tension or question rather than only a theme? Note which idea became clearer after the retry.
- **Support / extension:** Provide a partially completed grid or let students speak from keywords. Extend by testing the same idea against a skeptical audience.

### Week 3 — Two topic pitches

- **Objective:** Make two defensible topic pitches that show why the issue matters and what original perspective the student could contribute.
- **Materials / prompt:** Pitch template (question, perspective, audience, significance, possible payoff), timer, and listener response slips.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach a concise pitch: “My question is… I see it differently because… This matters to… I want them to leave thinking…” Students prepare two pitches, deliver each to a small audience, and answer one clarifying question. Feedback targets specificity and audience value; students revise and re-pitch one.
- **Speaking output:** Two 90-second topic pitches and a 30-second response to a listener’s question.
- **Proposed homework:** Draft both pitches in 200–300 words or keywords, then record one spoken version of each. Mark the sentence that is the central perspective and bring one possible backup topic.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can listeners tell the two topics apart, name the perspective, and explain why either could sustain a five-minute speech?
- **Support / extension:** Use sentence stems and a rehearsal partner. Extend by adding one counter-view without abandoning the student’s own position.

### Week 4 — Selection, backup, and baseline diagnosis

- **Objective:** Select a primary topic and backup topic with reasons, then set a practical baseline for content, structure, and delivery.
- **Materials / prompt:** Selection conference sheet, topic evidence checklist, timer, and a short diagnostic rubric separated into content, reasoning, delivery, interaction, and access.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach how to make a provisional decision without treating it as an identity choice. Students give a final 2-minute pitch for each candidate, compare research access and audience relevance, and choose one plus a backup. They revisit a baseline excerpt; coach gives one target in each relevant dimension and students retry the excerpt.
- **Speaking output:** Chosen topic + backup, written rationale, and a 3–5-minute baseline or equivalent diagnostic excerpts.
- **Proposed homework:** Gather three credible starting sources or source leads for the primary topic and write a one-sentence provisional message. Keep the backup rationale on the same page.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Is the selection supported by evidence of access, depth, and audience fit? Record timing and the first measurable target for the next phase.
- **Support / extension:** Offer a conference, a topic swap within the approved scan, or planning in a stronger language before English rehearsal. Extend by mapping a possible opposing interpretation.

## Weeks 5–8 — Message, structure, wording, and research

### Week 5 — Central message and the audience journey

- **Objective:** Turn the selected topic into one central message with a deliberate audience journey from question to payoff.
- **Materials / prompt:** Message test (“After five minutes, what should listeners think differently?”), arc map, and two sample openings.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach the difference between subject, thesis, and takeaway. Students write a one-sentence message, map two or three developments, and test three openings on partners. Partners state the message they heard; students revise the least clear beat and retry the opening plus close.
- **Speaking output:** One-sentence message, arc map, and three 45-second opening/close combinations.
- **Proposed homework:** Choose the clearest opening, draft the message and two main developments in keywords, and rehearse the opening and ending twice with a pause between them.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can a listener predict the speech’s direction from the opening without being given the outline?
- **Support / extension:** Supply opening patterns (question, image, contradiction). Extend by writing two different endings that answer “so what?”

### Week 6 — Structure, transitions, and explanation

- **Objective:** Build a coherent first section using claims, evidence or examples, explanation, and transitions.
- **Materials / prompt:** Claim–evidence–reasoning grid, transition bank, and prompt: “What does this prove, and why should this audience care?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a paragraph where evidence is present but the reasoning bridge is missing. Students draft one section, tell it from notes, and partners name the claim and evidence. Coach targets one missing link or transition; students add it and perform the section again.
- **Speaking output:** A 90–120-second section delivered from structured notes, with two explicit transitions.
- **Proposed homework:** Add one explanation sentence after each major piece of evidence and record the section once. Listen back for a place where the audience might ask “why?”
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Does each example serve the message, and does the speaker explain its significance rather than merely list it?
- **Support / extension:** Use color-coded claim/evidence/reason cards. Extend by adding a carefully framed alternative view.

### Week 7 — Authentic wording and note-based delivery

- **Objective:** Convert researched ideas into wording the student can own and deliver flexibly from keywords rather than a dense script.
- **Materials / prompt:** Source notes, paraphrase/original-wording checklist, cue-card template, and “say it once for a friend” exercise.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach the difference between quoting, paraphrasing, and copying; model reducing a paragraph to five cue words. Students explain one source in everyday language, build cue cards, and deliver the section twice. Feedback targets one unnatural phrase or note dependency; students rewrite and retry.
- **Speaking output:** Two-minute note-based explanation with source attribution where needed and no requirement to memorize every word.
- **Proposed homework:** Reduce the section to keywords, practise one take while looking only at notes, and highlight any phrase that does not sound like the student’s natural wording for revision.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can the speaker preserve the idea accurately while using accessible, authentic language and recovering from a glance at notes?
- **Support / extension:** Permit a fuller outline or audio planning. Extend by trying the same point for a younger or less familiar audience.

### Week 8 — Research expansion and near-five-minute checkpoint

- **Objective:** Expand the speech with enough credible context and analysis to approach five minutes without padding.
- **Materials / prompt:** Source log, evidence-selection checklist, full arc map, timer, and listener message slips.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach selection over accumulation: context, relevant evidence, explanation, and personal or audience link. Students assemble a rough complete speech, perform in pairs, and receive one content and one delivery observation. They cut repetition or add needed reasoning, then retry the weakest section and update the full outline.
- **Speaking output:** First near-five-minute checkpoint from notes, with a clear opening, progression, and close.
- **Proposed homework:** Revise the outline to fit the message, verify source details, and complete one timed rehearsal. Write down the exact section that ran short or long and why.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Record time, message clarity, analytical sufficiency, and where the audience connection weakened; treat this as developmental evidence, not placement.
- **Support / extension:** Break the speech into three rehearsable chunks. Extend by testing whether one source can be replaced with a stronger, more relevant source.

## Weeks 9–12 — Audience relevance, rhetoric, humor, and revision

### Week 9 — Audience relevance and shared situations

- **Objective:** Make the speech usable to listeners who do not share the speaker’s exact background or experience.
- **Materials / prompt:** Audience profile cards, unfamiliar-term check, and question: “What can this listener recognize, imagine, or take away?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach audience adaptation without flattening the student’s identity. Students explain a key idea to two different audience profiles, identify jargon or assumed context, and revise one example or bridge. Partners paraphrase what they understood; students retry the adapted passage.
- **Speaking output:** Two 90-second versions of the same point for different audiences, followed by a chosen version in the speech.
- **Proposed homework:** Add one listener bridge and one clarified term to the draft; rehearse the revised paragraph aloud twice and ask a listener what the point means to them.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Did the adaptation improve access while preserving the central message and the student’s own perspective?
- **Support / extension:** Provide audience profiles and vocabulary support. Extend by writing a bridge that acknowledges a likely difference in experience.

### Week 10 — Conversational language and rhetorical craft

- **Objective:** Use rhetorical questions, contrast, repetition, and callbacks deliberately so the speech sounds connected rather than decorated.
- **Materials / prompt:** Rhetorical device cards, before/after examples, and a marking key for purpose (orient, deepen, turn, or close).
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach one device at a time and ask what job it performs. Students mark the draft, test one rhetorical question and one contrast in delivery, and listen for whether the device clarifies the turn. Feedback targets purpose and naturalness; students remove or revise one device and retry.
- **Speaking output:** A 2-minute rhetorical passage with two labeled devices and an explanation of each device’s purpose.
- **Proposed homework:** Keep only the rhetorical choices that sharpen meaning, mark pauses and emphasis, and record the passage once from notes.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Do rhetorical choices make the idea easier to follow and remember, or do they distract from the reasoning?
- **Support / extension:** Offer a small choice of devices rather than requiring all of them. Extend by building a callback that genuinely changes meaning at the close.

### Week 11 — Appropriate humor and respectful risk testing

- **Objective:** Test whether a humorous moment serves connection or insight while protecting audience dignity and the student’s control of tone.
- **Materials / prompt:** Humor test (purpose, target, audience, consequence, alternative), consent-aware peer response, and neutral backup wording.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach that humor is optional and context-dependent. Students identify the intended function of a humorous line, perform it to a small audience, and collect responses about clarity and comfort. Coach helps keep, revise, or remove it; students deliver the passage again with a serious alternative ready.
- **Speaking output:** A 60–90-second passage with an optional humorous choice and a clean non-humorous version.
- **Proposed homework:** Choose the version that best serves the message, rehearse its transition into the next point twice, and note the audience condition under which the choice works.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Is the humor relevant, respectful, and understandable to this audience? Never treat willingness to disclose private or distressing material as a requirement.
- **Support / extension:** Use observational or wordplay options, or omit humor entirely. Extend by practising recovery if a line receives no response.

### Week 12 — Formal five-minute developmental checkpoint

- **Objective:** Deliver and revise a formal five-minute developmental version with sufficient content, analysis, audience connection, and preparation evidence.
- **Materials / prompt:** Timer, checkpoint rubric, notes, and structured listener form covering message, reasoning, audience, delivery, and recovery.
- **Lesson sequence:** Students perform the complete speech under formal conditions. Listeners record the message, strongest analytical link, least clear transition, and one delivery moment. Coach conferences prioritize one high-impact change; students revise a section and perform that section again, preserving evidence of both attempts.
- **Speaking output:** Formal five-minute checkpoint plus a written revision decision naming what changed and why.
- **Proposed homework:** Apply the chosen revision, update notes and source log, and complete one timed rehearsal. Bring a short reflection on what the audience understood without explanation.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Use the complete performance as developmental evidence; assess timing, analysis, connection, preparation, and the student’s use of feedback separately.
- **Support / extension:** Permit a trusted small audience or recorded first take. Extend by adding a question-and-response after the speech.

## Weeks 13–16 — Voice, movement, recovery, rehearsal, and readiness

### Week 13 — Individual voice clinic

- **Objective:** Make vocal choices—diction, projection, pace, pause, pitch, and tone—serve the meaning and the room.
- **Materials / prompt:** Short voice passages, listener-distance test, timer, and audio recording with consent.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach that intelligibility is audience access, not a demand to erase accent or identity. Students perform one passage from near and far positions, test a pause and emphasis change, and compare listener comprehension. Coach selects one voice target; students rerecord or repeat the passage with that target.
- **Speaking output:** A 90-second voice clinic passage and an annotated score showing purposeful pauses and emphasis.
- **Proposed homework:** Rehearse the selected voice target in one speech section and record one private take for self-review; keep the source recording private and follow the agreed deletion practice.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Could listeners hear and follow the key terms and message? Record the observable change in pace, phrasing, volume, or pause.
- **Support / extension:** Offer audio-only review, a microphone/room adjustment, or slower chunking. Extend by varying tone across the speech’s turn.

### Week 14 — Purposeful movement and recovery

- **Objective:** Use physical presence and recovery choices to support the message without forcing a fixed eye-contact or personality style.
- **Materials / prompt:** Simple stage map, cue cards, interruption/recovery prompts, and optional muted-video review with consent.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teach movement as a meaning decision: settle, shift at a structural turn, orient to listeners, and return to a stable position. Students map two purposeful movements, perform a section, and practise a missed word, lost place, or unexpected question. Feedback targets one physical choice and one recovery phrase; students retry under a small pressure change.
- **Speaking output:** A 2-minute section with marked movement beats and one deliberate recovery.
- **Proposed homework:** Rehearse the mapped movement and recovery cue once from notes, then write the simplest sentence that can restart the next idea.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Did movement clarify structure, and did the recovery preserve meaning and audience connection?
- **Support / extension:** Permit seated or low-movement delivery and alternate visual focus. Extend with a planned audience question after the section.

### Week 15 — Full rehearsal and pressure reliability

- **Objective:** Demonstrate a reliable complete speech through repeated full rehearsals, targeted revision, and realistic pressure conditions.
- **Materials / prompt:** Timer, final outline and source log, performance setup, listener rubric, and one controlled pressure variable.
- **Lesson sequence:** Students complete a full rehearsal, receive only the highest-impact feedback, and revise. After a short reset, they perform again with one pressure variable (new listener, standing setup, or question at the end). Compare timing, message, preparation, connection, delivery, and recovery across attempts; students choose the final repair.
- **Speaking output:** Two complete rehearsals near five minutes, with a brief evidence-based comparison.
- **Proposed homework:** Make the final structural or wording revision, confirm notes and sources, and complete one timed rehearsal under the chosen setup. Prepare a one-sentence response to “What is your message?”
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Is performance reliable across attempts, and is the student applying coaching rather than merely surviving one take?
- **Support / extension:** Reduce the pressure variable, shorten the feedback window, or rehearse in chunks before recombining. Extend with a listener unfamiliar with the topic.

### Week 16 — Final performance and written readiness recommendation

- **Objective:** Present the final five-minute Original Oratory and document course progress and readiness evidence separately.
- **Materials / prompt:** Performance setup, timer, final notes/source log, assessment record, and readiness checklist.
- **Lesson sequence:** Students perform the complete speech, answer a brief audience question where appropriate, and complete a reflection naming one successful choice and one continuing target. Coach records evidence across the course, gives one final usable next step, and writes a readiness recommendation based on multiple performances rather than one moment.
- **Speaking output:** Final reliable five-minute speech, audience connection, and written reflection.
- **Proposed homework:** Archive the final outline and reflection, then choose one maintenance rehearsal or future speaking goal. Any later practice should use the same specific target-and-retry cycle.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Assess final speech (45%), strongest complete developmental checkpoint (25%), consistency across analytical and OO tasks (20%), and preparation/revision/reliability/application of coaching (10%). Readiness additionally requires acceptable timing, sufficient content and analysis, live audience connection, and reliable preparation.
- **Support / extension:** Offer an accessible performance route agreed in advance and record the same learning evidence. Extension is a post-course refinement goal, never a promise of selection.

## Assessment and placement guardrails

Course progress is separate from nationals readiness. The weights above describe course evidence; they do not create a selection guarantee. A readiness recommendation uses the full record, including timing, content and analysis, audience connection, preparation, revision, and reliability. Students choose what personal material to use; no private or distressing disclosure, fixed personality, accent, constant eye contact, or particular humor style is required.
