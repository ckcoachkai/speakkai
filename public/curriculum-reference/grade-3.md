# Grade 3 — Stories, Interests, and Solutions: Weekly Plan

Fall 2026 · 16 weeks · proposed teaching plan

This plan expands the source curriculum into teachable weekly steps. It preserves the four source phases: personal-growth stories (Weeks 1–4), dramatic storytelling (Weeks 5–8), guided personal-interest research (Weeks 9–12), and integration/rehearsal/showcase (Weeks 13–16). The shared final target is a clear two-minute speech; a ready student may extend it to three minutes. Examples and research topics are prompts, not assigned historical homework.

## At a glance

| Weeks | Phase | Main question | Evidence of progress |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Personal-growth stories | What changed, and what can the listener learn? | Baseline, structured 1–2 minute growth story |
| 5–8 | Dramatic storytelling | How can delivery make a scene vivid? | Dramatic retellings and midpoint performance |
| 9–12 | Interest research and solutions | What is interesting, what problem does it raise, and why does it matter? | Source notes, fact/opinion check, researched sections |
| 13–16 | Integration and showcase | How can story, evidence, and delivery serve one audience? | Integrated drafts, full rehearsals, final two-minute speech |

## Adaptable proposed class framework

The source does not specify a class length. The following is a proposed 120-minute framework and can be shortened by protecting the model, one attempt, feedback, and retry. A teacher may use the break at a locally appropriate point.

| Minutes | Routine |
|---:|---|
| 0–10 | Retrieval warm-up: voice, vocabulary, story or reasoning prompt; state one observable target. |
| 10–25 | Teacher model and think-aloud; identify what the listener can hear or see. |
| 25–45 | Guided construction: shared example, prompt cards, source check, or partner rehearsal. |
| 45–55 | First student attempt in a small section or short speech. |
| 55–65 | Break and reset. |
| 65–90 | Individual drafting/rehearsal with cue cards or teacher conference. |
| 90–110 | Perform, receive one prioritized observation, revise, and retry. |
| 110–120 | Exit evidence: retell the target, save a draft/recording, and name the next practice step. |

## Phase 1 — Personal-growth stories

### Week 1 — A change worth telling

- **Objective:** Establish a baseline and identify a story with a clear change: what the speaker could not do, understand, or manage before, and what changed afterward.
- **Materials / prompt:** Two-column “before/after” card; picture sequence; prompt: “Tell us about learning, trying, or discovering something.” A fictional character or invented child may be used instead of personal disclosure.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a 45-second story with a beginning, change, and takeaway; jointly label the three beats; students make a keyword card; each gives a 60–90-second baseline; listener repeats the change they heard; speaker retries the opening and change with one clearer detail.
- **Speaking output:** Baseline growth story, 60–90 seconds, with a beginning, change, and ending message.
- **Proposed homework:** Choose a real, observed, fictional, or invented growth moment. Write five keywords—before, event, difficulty, change, lesson—and tell the story aloud once to a listener or recording.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can a listener state what changed and when it happened without teacher explanation?
- **Support / extension:** Offer picture cards, sentence stems (“At first… Then… Now…”), planning in keywords, or audio rehearsal; extend by adding one sensory detail and a specific takeaway.

### Week 2 — Character, setting, and specific detail

- **Objective:** Build a listener’s picture through character action, place, time, and sensory details instead of broad labels.
- **Materials / prompt:** Character–setting–detail grid; familiar object or image; prompt: “Show the moment through what the character notices and does.”
- **Lesson sequence:** Compare “It was hard” with a concrete action and sensory version; teacher models selecting two useful details; partners fill the grid; students retell the baseline story with a named character and setting; partner identifies the clearest image; speaker replaces one vague phrase and retries.
- **Speaking output:** 75–105-second story opening and middle with a clear character, setting, and two relevant details.
- **Proposed homework:** Add two sensory/action details to the keyword plan. Rehearse the first 45 seconds aloud twice, checking that the listener can picture where the scene occurs.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Does each detail reveal the scene or character’s experience, rather than merely add decoration?
- **Support / extension:** Provide a word bank and image choices; allow drawing before speaking; extend by varying one detail to show the character’s viewpoint.

### Week 3 — Conflict, climax, solution, and message

- **Objective:** Organize events around a problem and turning point, then make the solution and lesson understandable.
- **Materials / prompt:** Five-box story map: character, setting, conflict, climax, solution/message; prompt: “What stopped the character, and what choice changed the result?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a story map and mark the highest-stakes moment; students sequence mixed-up events; pairs ask “What was the problem?” and “What changed?”; students tell a 90-second version; teacher gives one structure cue; speaker moves the climax or message and retries.
- **Speaking output:** 90–120-second structured growth story containing conflict, climax, solution, and a stated or strongly implied message.
- **Proposed homework:** Complete the five-box map and rehearse once from keywords. Mark the sentence that names the turning point and the sentence that answers “What can the listener learn?”
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can the listener identify the problem, turning point, and result in the intended order?
- **Support / extension:** Use sentence strips and “because/so” links; accept a fictional story; extend by foreshadowing the turning point without giving away the result.

### Week 4 — Growth story draft and phase check

- **Objective:** Combine story structure and concrete detail into a coherent 1–2 minute growth story and record evidence for the next phase.
- **Materials / prompt:** Keyword outline, simple listener checklist, optional local audio recording; prompt: “What did the experience change, and why might this audience care?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Teacher models a concise opening and close; students rehearse section by section; each performs a first complete draft; listener reports the change and message; speaker receives one content or organization target, revises, and performs the draft again; teacher records baseline-to-retry evidence.
- **Speaking output:** Complete 1–2 minute growth story, with beginning, vivid details, conflict/climax, solution, and message.
- **Proposed homework:** Prepare the current story on one keyword card. Make one full audio rehearsal, then write two notes: the clearest moment and the one place a listener may need help.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Does the second attempt make the change and message easier to follow while keeping the story within 1–2 minutes?
- **Support / extension:** Permit a picture sequence, trusted listener, or audio-only submission; extend by trimming repetition and making the ending echo the opening image.

## Phase 2 — Dramatic storytelling

### Week 5 — Narrator and character voices

- **Objective:** Distinguish narration from dialogue and choose understandable voice changes that serve the scene.
- **Materials / prompt:** Short teacher-created scene with narrator and two characters; color-coded narration/dialogue cards; prompt: “Who is speaking, and what does each voice need the audience to know?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Model the same line as narration and dialogue; students mark speaker changes; choral and partner read; each performs a 30–45-second scene; listener identifies narrator and characters; speaker retries with clearer phrasing and transitions.
- **Speaking output:** 45–60-second dramatic excerpt with audible narrator/character distinction.
- **Proposed homework:** Mark three narration lines and three dialogue lines in the story. Rehearse them aloud once, changing voice only where it helps the listener identify the speaker.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can a listener follow who is speaking without seeing the script?
- **Support / extension:** Use icons, color coding, or a single character plus narrator; extend by making dialogue reveal a goal or problem rather than repeat narration.

### Week 6 — Emotion, gesture, and show-not-tell

- **Objective:** Communicate emotion through word choice, facial/body action, and vocal choices without forcing a fixed personality or performance style.
- **Materials / prompt:** Emotion/action cards; neutral sentence; prompt: “Show the character’s feeling through what they do, say, and notice.”
- **Lesson sequence:** Teacher models telling versus showing; students choose an action and a vocal cue; pairs perform the same line with two emotions; students revise one story scene; partner names the evidence of emotion; speaker retries with one purposeful gesture and one precise verb.
- **Speaking output:** 60–75-second scene in which emotion is shown through language, voice, and a purposeful action.
- **Proposed homework:** Replace two feeling labels with observable actions or precise verbs. Rehearse the scene twice, keeping one gesture that supports meaning and removing movements that distract.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can the audience infer the emotion from observable choices, and do the choices preserve intelligibility?
- **Support / extension:** Offer seated gestures, object-based action, or audio-only voice practice; extend by contrasting the character’s outward action with an unstated inner feeling.

### Week 7 — Pace, pause, emphasis, and transitions

- **Objective:** Use pauses and phrasing to separate events, spotlight the turning point, and keep the dramatic story understandable.
- **Materials / prompt:** Printed excerpt with slash marks and emphasis underlines; timer; prompt: “Where should the audience have time to picture, worry, or notice a change?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Teacher performs a rushed and a shaped version; students mark pause points and one key word per beat; partner rehearsal; students record or perform a 60–90-second section; listener points to the turning point heard most clearly; speaker adjusts one pace or pause choice and retries.
- **Speaking output:** 75–105-second dramatic section with purposeful pause, pace, emphasis, and transitions.
- **Proposed homework:** Mark three pauses and two emphasis words on the keyword card. Rehearse once slowly for clarity and once at performance pace; keep the version whose meaning is easiest to follow.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Do the pauses clarify the sequence and give the key moment time to land without making the delivery fragmented?
- **Support / extension:** Use a metronome-like tap, teacher echo, or shorter excerpt; extend by varying pace between calm narration and an urgent moment.

### Week 8 — Dramatic story midpoint performance

- **Objective:** Deliver a complete dramatic story and use audience evidence to make one focused revision before research work begins.
- **Materials / prompt:** Keyword card, private recording option, listener card with “scene / speaker / turning point / clearest choice”; prompt: “Which delivery choice helps this audience experience the story?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Warm up articulation and breath; teacher models a complete opening-to-close arc; students perform a 1–2 minute dramatic story; listener states the scene and turning point and gives one observable delivery note; speaker revises one voice, gesture, pace, or pause choice; second performance is compared with the first.
- **Speaking output:** Complete 1–2 minute dramatic story performance; midpoint evidence includes first attempt, prioritized feedback, and retry.
- **Proposed homework:** Select the strongest version of the story and write a short note identifying one delivery choice to carry into the research speech. Rehearse the opening and turning point once from keywords.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** After the retry, can the audience identify the story arc and the purposeful delivery choice?
- **Support / extension:** Perform first to one listener, submit audio, or use a narrated slide sequence; extend by adding a controlled character exchange while keeping the message clear.

## Phase 3 — Guided interest research and solutions

### Week 9 — Choose an interest and ask a useful question

- **Objective:** Turn a broad interest into an audience-friendly question that can be explored with teacher-guided sources.
- **Materials / prompt:** Topic menu (animals, space, sports, art, music, technology, games, nature, health, transportation, inventions, or historical mysteries); question funnel; teacher-approved source set.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model narrowing “space” to a question; students brainstorm three interests and audience possibilities; teacher checks age-appropriate scope and sources; pairs convert a topic into “How/why/what happens when…?” questions; each student pitches one question and receives one feasibility note; student selects a working question or a teacher-provided alternative.
- **Speaking output:** 30–45-second topic pitch stating interest, audience, and research question.
- **Proposed homework:** Bring the approved question and three keywords. Explain aloud what the audience might learn and record one question you still need to answer; use only teacher-approved materials.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Is the question specific enough to answer with a small number of checked sources and relevant to a real audience?
- **Support / extension:** Offer a narrowed question bank and shared source; extend by proposing two audiences and explaining how the explanation would change.

### Week 10 — Facts, opinions, and source notes

- **Objective:** Distinguish a checkable fact from an opinion, record source details, and paraphrase useful information in the student’s own words.
- **Materials / prompt:** Two or three teacher-guided sources per topic; fact/opinion cards; source-note template with title/link or identifying detail; prompt: “What does the source say, and how do you know what kind of statement it is?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Model checking a claim and paraphrasing it without copying; students sort statements; teacher and students inspect a source together; each records two relevant facts and one opinion or interpretation; partner asks “How could we check that?”; student orally paraphrases one fact and corrects any unsupported wording.
- **Speaking output:** 45–60-second research update with two accurately paraphrased facts and a clearly labeled opinion/interpretation.
- **Proposed homework:** Complete two source notes in your own words and rehearse a 45-second update. Bring the source identifiers and circle the fact that best supports your topic question.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can the student identify which statements are checkable, name the source, and explain the fact without reading it word for word?
- **Support / extension:** Provide a shared highlighted excerpt, vocabulary glossary, or home-language planning; extend by comparing two sources’ wording and identifying a question they leave open.

### Week 11 — Personal connection, problem, and importance

- **Objective:** Connect the interest to a concrete problem and explain why the problem matters to the intended audience.
- **Materials / prompt:** Interest → connection → problem → affected people/place organizer; audience cards; prompt: “Who or what is affected, and what would happen if the problem were ignored?” A personal connection may be an observation, a question, or an imagined viewpoint; disclosure is optional.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model moving from a fact to a problem and consequence; students map their facts to affected groups or places; teacher checks that claims are supported and language is respectful; partners ask “Why does this matter to us?”; students deliver a 60–75-second problem section; partner reports the problem and importance heard; student adds one missing link and retries.
- **Speaking output:** 60–75-second section stating the problem, who/what it affects, and why it matters.
- **Proposed homework:** Finish the organizer with one source-supported fact about impact and three keywords explaining why the audience should care. Rehearse the section once aloud.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Does the speech explain the link from interest to problem to importance, rather than simply list facts?
- **Support / extension:** Use an invented case or community example instead of personal experience; extend by acknowledging that different groups may experience the problem differently.

### Week 12 — Solution, message, and researched speech sections

- **Objective:** Propose a realistic response or message, connect it to the problem, and assemble a short researched section with a clear structure.
- **Materials / prompt:** Problem–importance–solution frame; solution test (feasible, relevant, supported, audience-appropriate); keyword outline; prompt: “What could be done, by whom, and what should the audience remember?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Compare a vague wish with a specific response; students test two possible solutions against the frame; teacher models a transition from evidence to proposal; students draft opening, facts, problem, importance, and solution sections; partner identifies the message and any unsupported leap; student revises and delivers a 90–120-second section.
- **Speaking output:** 90–120-second researched speech section with an opening question or message, facts in own words, problem, importance, solution, and a clear close.
- **Proposed homework:** Assemble a one-page keyword outline and rehearse the full researched section once. Highlight the transition into the solution and write the source identifiers beneath the relevant facts.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can the listener state the proposed solution and why it responds to the problem, while the student keeps fact and opinion distinct?
- **Support / extension:** Supply transition stems and a teacher-approved solution menu; extend by comparing two responses and explaining the tradeoff without exceeding the student’s language control.

## Phase 4 — Integration, rehearsal, and showcase

### Week 13 — Integrate story, research, and audience purpose

- **Objective:** Build one two-minute arc that uses a story or scene to open the topic, evidence to explain it, and a solution or message to close.
- **Materials / prompt:** Story and research keyword cards; four-beat outline (opening/story, interest/facts, problem/importance, solution/message); audience card; prompt: “Why should this audience listen from the first sentence to the last?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Teacher models a concise integrated speech and marks transitions; students choose which story detail genuinely serves the research message; guided sequencing; first complete 90–120-second attempt; listener names the opening, central message, and close; speaker cuts repetition or adds one missing bridge and retries.
- **Speaking output:** First integrated 1.5–2 minute draft with a purposeful story-to-research transition.
- **Proposed homework:** Write the four-beat keyword outline and rehearse the complete draft once. Ask a listener to state the message in one sentence and record their exact answer.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Does every story detail or fact support the central message and intended audience?
- **Support / extension:** Use a supplied opening or visual sequence; extend by selecting a second audience and making one vocabulary or example change for it.

### Week 14 — Clarity, rhetoric, and audience questions

- **Objective:** Revise wording and transitions for a real audience, then answer a relevant question using evidence or a transparent limitation.
- **Materials / prompt:** Draft, transition bank, question cards, fact/source notes; prompt: “What might this audience ask, and how can you answer without pretending to know more than the source shows?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a claim–reason–example bridge and a respectful “I would need to check that” response; students underline the central message and transition words; partner performs audience question; speaker answers in 20–30 seconds; partner reports whether the answer connected to the speech; student revises one phrase and retries the question.
- **Speaking output:** Revised 2-minute draft plus one 20–30-second audience response.
- **Proposed homework:** Prepare three likely questions and keyword answers, including one question that requires checking the source. Rehearse the speech once, pausing before the main message.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Does the student answer the question directly, distinguish evidence from opinion, and return to the speech’s purpose?
- **Support / extension:** Offer question stems and allow a source card; extend by answering a respectful alternative viewpoint without dismissing it.

### Week 15 — Full rehearsal, recovery, and optional extension

- **Objective:** Deliver a reliable two-minute speech, recover from a missed word or question, and decide whether a controlled extension toward three minutes is ready.
- **Materials / prompt:** Keyword card, timer, private recording option, recovery stems (“Let me restate that…”, “The key point is…”), readiness checklist; prompt: “Which choice keeps the audience with you when something goes wrong?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Teacher models a pause-and-restart recovery; students run a timed full rehearsal; partner checks message, facts, structure, delivery, and response; speaker chooses one target, retries the weakest section, then runs the full speech again; teacher checks whether an extension adds explanation rather than repetition.
- **Speaking output:** Timed two-minute full rehearsal; optional 2.5–3 minute version only when content, organization, and delivery remain clear.
- **Proposed homework:** Complete one timed rehearsal from keywords and make one revision. If extending, add one supported explanation or audience example and check that the speech remains purposeful.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Can the student maintain the message, recover calmly, and finish within the chosen time without reading a script?
- **Support / extension:** Use a seated/audio rehearsal, shorter cue card, or trusted first audience; extend with one counter-question, comparison, or consequence supported by notes.

### Week 16 — Final integrated speech and reflection

- **Objective:** Present the integrated speech for the intended audience, demonstrate use of feedback, and reflect on the next speaking step.
- **Materials / prompt:** Final keyword card and source notes; timer; audience/listener response card; reflection prompts: “What changed after feedback?” and “What will you practise next?”
- **Lesson sequence:** Quiet preparation and pronunciation check; each student gives the final two-minute speech, with optional extension to three minutes when ready; audience records the central message and one memorable choice; student answers one question or gives a recovery response if needed; teacher and student compare baseline, midpoint, and final evidence; student completes reflection.
- **Speaking output:** Final integrated two-minute speech connecting interest/story, checked facts, problem, importance, and possible solution/message; extension up to three minutes when it adds meaningful content.
- **Proposed homework:** Save the final outline and write a short reflection naming one observable improvement, one remaining target, and one future speaking situation in which to use the skill.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Does the final performance show a clear message, accurate guided research, purposeful dramatic delivery, audience connection, preparation, and an observable response to earlier feedback?
- **Support / extension:** Use an audio or small-audience route where appropriate and documented; extend by answering audience questions or comparing how the message changes for a second audience.

## Evidence and assessment notes

Use comparable evidence at Week 1 baseline, Week 8 midpoint, and Week 16 final. Record the task, support conditions, duration, observable content/structure, delivery choices, audience response, feedback target, and result of the retry. Keep content/reasoning, delivery, language clarity, interaction, and access supports distinct. A polished report should describe what was observed and the next rehearsable action; it should not infer confidence, ability, motivation, or personality from a single performance.

The shared final target is two minutes. Evaluate whether the speech has sufficient content, a clear problem–importance–solution or message arc, accurate use of guided sources, understandable language, purposeful dramatic choices, preparation, and application of feedback. A three-minute version is an extension route for a student who can add relevant explanation without losing clarity.
