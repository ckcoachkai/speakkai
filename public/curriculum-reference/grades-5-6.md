# Grades 5–6 — Proposed 16-Week Course Outline

Fall 2026 | 16 weeks | Final target: a polished 3–4-minute analytical expository speech

This is a proposed teaching plan derived from the supplied [Grades 5–6 curriculum](../grades-5-6.md), the [speaking-feedback research](../../../research/07-speaking-feedback.md), and the [practical playbook](../../../PLAYBOOK.md). It expands the source phases into teachable weekly progressions. The weekly homework below is proposed for planning; it is not a record of historical assignments. The source outline does not specify a class length, so the session framework is an adaptable 120-minute example rather than a claim about the actual schedule.

## Course arc

| Weeks | Proposed phase | Main question | Portfolio checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Content I | What is the issue, what causes it, and what should the audience understand? | Issue map, claim, opening, causes section |
| 5–8 | Presentation I | How can the speaker make organized thinking easy to hear? | Structured notes and 2–3-minute midpoint presentation |
| 9–12 | Content II | Who is affected, what are the impacts, which perspectives matter, and what could help? | Complete analytical outline and key sections |
| 13–16 | Presentation II | How can the speaker sound natural, adapt, recover, and connect with listeners? | Rehearsed 3–4-minute final speech |

Representative topic lanes include advertising, online identity, trends, language and culture, school habits, group influence, fairness, inclusion, community responsibility, changing traditions, and consumer behavior. Students may change topics as relevance and source quality develop. No private or distressing disclosure is required.

## Adaptable proposed 120-minute session framework

Use the same visible rhythm each week while changing the task. Adjust timings for the actual class length, attendance, language needs, access needs, and number of speakers.

1. **0–10 minutes — Retrieve and orient:** revisit one prior speaking move; state today’s audience, purpose, and success target.
2. **10–25 minutes — Model and notice:** teacher gives a short model, then students mark the claim, evidence, transition, vocal choice, or audience move they heard.
3. **25–50 minutes — Guided practice:** build or rehearse one small section with a partner, sentence frame, visual organizer, or cue card.
4. **50–60 minutes — Break:** pause and reset materials.
5. **60–85 minutes — First attempts:** students deliver a section or short talk to a partner, small group, or teacher; listeners record the message heard and one unclear point.
6. **85–105 minutes — Feedback and retry:** teacher gives one evidence-based action; students revise and perform the same section again.
7. **105–115 minutes — Portfolio reflection:** save the current outline, recording, cue card, or source log and write what changed between attempts.
8. **115–120 minutes — Homework and exit check:** student states the next practice action and what they will bring or demonstrate next time.

Feedback should name observable evidence, its effect on the listener, one priority action, and the next check. Keep content/reasoning, delivery, interaction, and access needs distinct. A recommendation is labelled as such; it is never presented as an observed result.

## Weeks 1–4 — Content I: context, message, openings, and causes

### Week 1 — Diagnostic: issue, audience, and purpose

- **Objective:** Identify a familiar social or cultural issue and state what a specific audience should know, feel, decide, or do; establish a baseline for message clarity, organization, evidence use, and delivery.
- **Materials / prompt:** Topic image or short neutral scenario cards: targeted advertising, school phone habits, changing slang, or a community tradition; audience card; one-page issue map; timer; optional audio recorder with consent.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a 60-second explanation of one issue and think aloud about audience and purpose. In pairs, students complete “The issue is… / My audience is… / I want them to understand…”. Students give a 60–90-second baseline. Listener writes the main message and one question. Teacher gives one observable next action; students repeat the opening and message.
- **Speaking output:** 60–90-second baseline explanation with a named issue, audience, and provisional takeaway.
- **Proposed homework:** Choose one topic lane, write a one-sentence purpose and three questions to investigate, and record or rehearse a 45–60-second explanation twice. Bring the question list and note which version made the purpose clearest.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Record whether the listener could state the issue and purpose without prompting; note one content or delivery priority for Week 2.
- **Support / extension:** Offer picture prompts, a bilingual planning glossary, or oral planning before English drafting. Extend by naming a likely audience assumption and adapting one example for a different audience.

### Week 2 — Context: background before judgment

- **Objective:** Build enough neutral background for an audience to understand the issue; separate description of context from an early opinion.
- **Materials / prompt:** Three short teacher-selected source excerpts or infographics on one safe topic; source log; “What is happening? Who is involved? Where/when?” organizer; highlighters.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model turning a source fact into a plain-language context sentence and label fact versus interpretation. Groups extract two relevant facts and explain unfamiliar terms. Students deliver a 90-second context section, then a partner paraphrases what they learned. Teacher targets either missing context or an unsupported judgment; students revise one sentence and retry.
- **Speaking output:** 90-second context section containing two relevant facts, explained vocabulary, and a clear bridge to the issue.
- **Proposed homework:** Add three source notes to the log: author or organization, date if available, exact fact, and why it matters to the audience. Rehearse the context section with only five keywords on a card.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check that each fact is traceable to a source and that the speaker explains its relevance instead of only listing it.
- **Support / extension:** Provide preselected accessible sources and sentence frames such as “This matters because…”. Extend by comparing two sources’ framing or noting a limitation without overloading the talk.

### Week 3 — Facts, opinions, and central idea

- **Objective:** Distinguish fact, opinion, inference, and question; form a defensible central idea that can organize the speech.
- **Materials / prompt:** Statement sort cards on advertising, online identity, school habits, or consumer behavior; claim–evidence–reasoning grid; source log; sticky notes.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model sorting “The ad shows a discount” (fact), “The ad is persuasive” (interpretation), and “The ad should be restricted” (opinion/claim), then demonstrate a reasoning bridge. Pairs sort cards and justify one disagreement. Students state a central idea and support it with one fact plus an explanation. Listener writes the central idea heard; students revise and repeat if the message is too broad.
- **Speaking output:** 90 seconds: central idea, one accurately labelled fact, and a reason explaining the connection.
- **Proposed homework:** Draft two possible central ideas and choose one after testing each on a family member or peer: “What do you think my main point is?” Add one source-backed fact and one sentence of reasoning.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Verify that the central idea is specific enough to guide two or three sections and that the fact is not being presented as proof without explanation.
- **Support / extension:** Use color coding for fact/opinion/reason and provide a claim stem. Extend by adding a carefully worded limitation or alternative interpretation.

### Week 4 — Openings and causes

- **Objective:** Create an opening that makes the issue and audience relevance clear, then explain at least two causes in a purposeful order.
- **Materials / prompt:** Opening menu (question, surprising contrast, brief scene, or concrete fact); cause-chain organizer; transition bank; timer.
- **Lesson sequence:** Compare two openings for a school-habits topic and identify which gives the audience a reason to listen. Groups build a cause chain with “because” and “which leads to” links. Students deliver an opening plus one cause section. Listener names the opening’s question and the cause order. Teacher gives one action such as “name the issue before the statistic” or “explain the link between causes”; students retry.
- **Speaking output:** 2 minutes: opening, central idea, and two cause-and-effect links with at least one relevant example or fact.
- **Proposed homework:** Draft a 2-minute opening-and-causes section using no more than six cue-card keywords per section. Rehearse once for structure and once for audible phrasing; mark the sentence that needs the clearest pause.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check whether a new listener can answer “What is this about?” and “Why is it happening?” after the section.
- **Support / extension:** Supply a partially completed cause chain or allow a spoken outline. Extend by distinguishing a root cause from a visible symptom and explaining why that distinction matters.

## Weeks 5–8 — Presentation I: delivery, audience, and structured notes

### Week 5 — Diction and projection for meaning

- **Objective:** Make essential words audible and understandable to the intended audience through phrasing, consonant clarity, and appropriate projection.
- **Materials / prompt:** Student opening-and-causes drafts; listener-distance markers; essential-term cards; optional audio recording; no accent imitation requirement.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model the same sentence with unclear phrasing and then with grouped meaning. Students mark slash points, practice key terms, and test volume from the listener’s position. Each student gives the opening and one cause. Listener identifies the clearest key term and one word or phrase that was hard to hear. Teacher assigns one change; student rerecords or retries.
- **Speaking output:** 2 minutes from structured notes, with clear phrasing of the central idea, key terms, and cause links.
- **Proposed homework:** Make a 30–45-second audio practice from the listener’s position. Listen once for audibility and once for key-term clarity; choose one phrase to re-record and write the change made.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Note whether the message remains understandable at natural volume and whether pronunciation coaching targets access to meaning rather than accent conformity.
- **Support / extension:** Allow a slower first take, a pronunciation guide for names, or an audio-only route. Extend by varying emphasis to distinguish a cause from an example.

### Week 6 — Expressive clarity: pace, pauses, and emphasis

- **Objective:** Use pace, pauses, pitch, and emphasis to show structure and give the audience time to process evidence and reasoning.
- **Materials / prompt:** Same speech section; pause-marking symbols; two short teacher models; metronome or timer only as an optional awareness tool.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a rushed paragraph and a meaning-grouped version. Students mark pauses before the central idea, after evidence, and at transitions. In pairs, they perform two takes and identify which idea landed. Teacher gives one target such as “pause after the fact before explaining why it matters”; students retry and compare.
- **Speaking output:** 2 minutes with at least three purposeful pauses, clear emphasis on the central idea, and a pace that preserves explanation.
- **Proposed homework:** Rehearse the section in two versions: natural pace and deliberately slower pace. Keep the version in which a listener can repeat the claim and one reason; note the pause that helped most.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check meaning effects rather than counting pauses; record whether listeners could identify the claim, evidence, and explanation.
- **Support / extension:** Provide a visual script with pause marks or permit hand tapping during practice. Extend by using a deliberate pace shift to signal a contrast or consequence.

### Week 7 — Audience address and structured-note speaking

- **Objective:** Speak from a concise outline while using inclusive language, relevant examples, orientation, and visual focus choices that help the audience follow.
- **Materials / prompt:** Six-keyword outline template; audience profile cards (classmates, younger pupils, school council); example bank; partner message-heard form.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model reading a paragraph versus speaking from six keywords. Students compress one section and adapt an example for two audiences. In triads, one speaks, one listens for audience connection, and one tracks note use. Teacher gives one content or delivery action; speaker retries without adding a script.
- **Speaking output:** 2–3 minutes from structured notes, including one audience question or shared situation and one adapted example.
- **Proposed homework:** Reduce the current draft to one card per main section, then rehearse once for a family member or peer. Ask, “What was my main point, and which example felt relevant?” Record the answers without correcting the listener.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check whether notes prompt ideas rather than become a script, and whether the audience move clarifies the issue instead of becoming decoration.
- **Support / extension:** Offer a larger-print card, icon cues, seated presentation, or a trusted listener. Extend by changing one example and explaining the audience choice.

### Week 8 — Midpoint presentation: 2–3 minutes

- **Objective:** Integrate the first four weeks of content and delivery into a coherent 2–3-minute midpoint presentation and use listener evidence to select the next revision priority.
- **Materials / prompt:** Current opening, central idea, causes, structured notes, source log, midpoint checklist; consent-based audio/video option; timer.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teacher models a concise run-through and names the success criteria: message, cause links, evidence explanation, audibility, structure, and audience connection. Students deliver the full 2–3-minute midpoint in pairs or small groups. Listeners write the message heard, strongest support, and least-clear link. Teacher gives one content priority and one delivery observation; students retry the weakest section, then archive the midpoint and reflection.
- **Speaking output:** Complete 2–3-minute analytical presentation from structured notes; this is the midpoint checkpoint, not the final assessment.
- **Proposed homework:** Review the midpoint notes and write a revision memo: keep one effective choice, repair one content gap, and choose one delivery target. Update the source log with any missing attribution before Week 9.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Record evidence separately for ideas/organization, evidence/reasoning, delivery, audience connection, preparation, and response to feedback; do not infer progress from confidence or personality.
- **Support / extension:** Allow audio or a smaller audience when live presentation creates an access barrier while preserving the same message and reasoning target. Extend by answering one neutral audience question after the talk.

## Weeks 9–12 — Content II: affected groups, impacts, perspectives, solutions

### Week 9 — Who is affected?

- **Objective:** Identify at least two affected groups and explain how the same issue may create different experiences or interests for each group.
- **Materials / prompt:** Stakeholder map; neutral scenario such as targeted advertising, school phone habits, changing traditions, or community responsibility; accessible source set; perspective sentence stems.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a stakeholder map without assigning motives: “Students may experience…, families may notice…, organizations may…”. Groups add evidence to two branches and identify missing information. Students deliver a 90-second affected-group comparison. Partner asks, “How do you know?” Teacher targets unsupported generalization or unclear comparison; students revise and retry.
- **Speaking output:** 90 seconds comparing two affected groups with one source-backed or clearly labelled example for each.
- **Proposed homework:** Add two stakeholder entries to the outline, each with group, possible effect, evidence or source, and a cautious wording check. Rehearse the comparison with one transition.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check that groups are described respectfully and that the student distinguishes evidence from speculation; flag any claim requiring further research.
- **Support / extension:** Provide group cards and “may / can / in this example” language. Extend by identifying a group whose perspective is absent and explaining what evidence would be needed.

### Week 10 — Impacts and evidence

- **Objective:** Explain short-term and longer-term impacts, selecting evidence that supports the central idea and adding reasoning between evidence and claim.
- **Materials / prompt:** Impact ladder; two or three teacher-reviewed sources; claim–evidence–reasoning grid; source reliability questions; calculator or simple chart only when needed.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model the bridge “This evidence matters because…”. Students place impact cards on a short/long-term ladder and test whether each belongs to the topic. Pairs build one evidence paragraph and speak it. Listener states the evidence and the conclusion they think follows. Teacher assigns one bridge sentence or source clarification; student retries.
- **Speaking output:** 2 minutes explaining one immediate and one wider impact, with two sources or examples and explicit reasoning.
- **Proposed homework:** Complete two claim–evidence–reasoning rows and verify each source’s author/organization and date when available. Rehearse the impact section with the evidence in notes, not on a full script.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check source traceability, relevance, and the explanation linking evidence to impact; do not reward statistics that the student cannot explain.
- **Support / extension:** Use a teacher-provided source bundle and sentence frames. Extend by qualifying a claim (“in this sample,” “the source suggests”) or comparing evidence strength.

### Week 11 — Perspectives and respectful complexity

- **Objective:** Represent a relevant alternative perspective accurately, explain a point of agreement or tension, and keep the student’s central idea visible.
- **Materials / prompt:** Two short contrasting perspectives on fairness, online identity, consumer behavior, or school habits; perspective chart; “Some people may…” and “The evidence suggests…” stems.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a fair paraphrase before responding to a view. Pairs identify each source’s concern, evidence, and assumption. Students give a 2-minute perspective section and respond to one clarifying question. Teacher targets misrepresentation, false balance, or an unsupported dismissal; student revises and retries.
- **Speaking output:** 2 minutes: accurate alternative perspective, evidence or reason behind it, and a reasoned response that returns to the central idea.
- **Proposed homework:** Write a 100–150-word perspective-and-response draft, then reduce it to eight keywords and rehearse it aloud twice. Ask a listener whether the alternative view sounded fair before hearing the response.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check whether the speaker can state the strongest version of the alternative view and whether the response uses reasoning rather than labels.
- **Support / extension:** Provide paraphrase stems and allow oral planning. Extend by identifying what new evidence could change the student’s conclusion.

### Week 12 — Solutions, personal relevance, and complete outline

- **Objective:** Propose realistic responses or solutions, explain trade-offs, connect the issue to a familiar but non-private situation, and assemble a complete analytical outline.
- **Materials / prompt:** Solution test: actor, action, mechanism, likely benefit, trade-off; full outline template; source log; audience takeaway cards.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model separating “raise awareness” from a specific action with an actor and mechanism. Groups test solution cards for feasibility and unintended effects. Students present their solution and personal-relevance bridge. Teacher gives one priority such as “explain how the action changes the cause” or “make the example concrete”; student retries. Students then arrange opening, context, causes, affected groups, impacts, perspectives, solution, and close into a full outline.
- **Speaking output:** 3 minutes of key sections plus a complete outline capable of supporting the final 3–4-minute speech.
- **Proposed homework:** Produce a complete structured outline with source notes and a 1–2-sentence closing that answers “So what?”. Rehearse the whole outline once and mark any section that runs long, feels unsupported, or is hard to explain without reading.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check that every major section serves the central idea, the solution has a mechanism and trade-off, and personal relevance remains appropriately bounded.
- **Support / extension:** Offer a fillable outline and a menu of solution verbs. Extend by proposing two solutions and comparing which better fits the audience and evidence.

## Weeks 13–16 — Presentation II: personality, flexibility, recovery, and final integration

### Week 13 — Vocal personality and purposeful movement

- **Objective:** Add an authentic vocal and physical presence that supports meaning, using variety without forcing a personality, gesture style, or eye-contact pattern.
- **Materials / prompt:** Complete outline; keyword cards; delivery map for emphasis, pause, orientation, and one purposeful gesture; listener-distance setup; optional audio/video with consent.
- **Lesson sequence:** Teacher models a flat and an overperformed version, then a grounded version with varied emphasis. Students mark the two ideas that deserve vocal contrast and choose a comfortable visual-focus route. They deliver a 2-minute section, receive one listener observation, and retry with one purposeful change.
- **Speaking output:** 2–3 minutes from notes with deliberate emphasis, pauses, clear orientation, and movement that supports a point or transition.
- **Proposed homework:** Record or rehearse the opening and close twice: once focusing on vocal emphasis and once on calm orientation. Keep one authentic choice from each take and write why it helps the audience.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Record the observable effect of vocal/physical choices on message access; never equate eye contact, stillness, or expressiveness with confidence or character.
- **Support / extension:** Permit seated delivery, a fixed visual focus, reduced movement, or audio-only first. Extend by using a contrast in pace or stance to distinguish problem from solution.

### Week 14 — Adaptable examples and audience questions

- **Objective:** Select examples that make the issue relevant to this audience, answer a content question, and adjust wording while preserving the central idea.
- **Materials / prompt:** Audience profile cards; example bank; question cards ranging from clarification to challenge; eight-keyword outline; response stems.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model replacing a generic example with one that fits younger pupils, classmates, or a school council. Students adapt one example for two audiences, then perform a section. Partners ask one question; speakers pause, answer the question directly, and bridge back to the message. Teacher targets either audience mismatch or a vague answer; students retry.
- **Speaking output:** 3 minutes including one audience-specific example and a 20–30-second spoken answer to a relevant question.
- **Proposed homework:** Prepare three likely audience questions and brief evidence-based answers. Rehearse answering from keywords, including a recovery phrase such as “The key point for this audience is…”.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check whether the example is relevant and safe, whether the answer addresses the actual question, and whether the speaker returns to the speech’s purpose.
- **Support / extension:** Give question stems and allow a short planning pause before answering. Extend by handling a respectful challenge or naming evidence that remains uncertain.

### Week 15 — Recovery and full rehearsal

- **Objective:** Recover from a lost place, forgotten word, timing change, or audience interruption while maintaining structure and completing a full 3–4-minute rehearsal.
- **Materials / prompt:** Complete notes; recovery cards (“pause and breathe,” “restate the point,” “use the next keyword”); timer; peer checklist; optional recording with consent.
- **Lesson sequence:** Model a calm recovery after losing a sentence and show how to return to the next keyword. Students practise short disrupted sections, then deliver full speeches in pairs or triads. Listeners note one recovery move and one point where structure or timing weakened. Teacher gives one high-impact action; students rerun the affected section and, when time permits, the full speech.
- **Speaking output:** Full 3–4-minute rehearsal from structured notes, including at least one planned recovery practice.
- **Proposed homework:** Complete one timed full rehearsal. Mark actual time, the section that needs trimming or expansion, one content gap, and one delivery target; revise the notes and rehearse the changed section once.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Check timing, completeness, evidence explanation, audience connection, note use, and recovery as separate observations; distinguish a missed rehearsal from an ability judgment.
- **Support / extension:** Use a smaller audience, audio submission, a visible outline, or a planned pause. Extend by answering an unplanned but relevant question after the timed speech.

### Week 16 — Final integration and reflection

- **Objective:** Deliver and reflect on a polished 3–4-minute analytical expository speech that explains an issue, uses evidence and perspectives, proposes a response, and connects with the audience.
- **Materials / prompt:** Final outline and cue cards; source log; timer; agreed feedback checklist; reflection form; private recording option with deletion plan if used.
- **Lesson sequence:** Students complete a quiet preparation check and state their audience and central idea. Each student delivers the final speech to the planned audience or an agreed access route. Listener or teacher records evidence for message, organization, evidence/reasoning, delivery, audience connection, preparation, and response to feedback. Student receives one concrete next step, reflects on the change from Week 1, and identifies a transferable speaking skill.
- **Speaking output:** Final 3–4-minute analytical expository speech with a clear opening, context, causes, affected groups, impacts, evidence, perspective, solution, audience-relevant example, and purposeful close; sections may be adapted when the topic’s evidence calls for a different order.
- **Proposed homework:** Write a brief reflection: what the audience understood, which revision most improved the speech, what evidence or delivery choice to keep, and one next speaking goal. Store or delete recordings according to the approved local process.
- **Feedback checkpoint:** Compare the final with the comparable midpoint evidence; report observable change and remaining priorities without claiming causation from the course alone. Preserve missing or uncertain evidence explicitly.
- **Support / extension:** Preserve the communication target while permitting audio, a trusted small audience, assistive technology, or a written question response where appropriate. Extend by adapting the same message into a 60-second version for a new audience.

## Portfolio and feedback record

For each student, retain only approved records: topic and audience, source log, outline versions, midpoint evidence, final evidence, one action from each feedback cycle, and the student’s reflection. Label each entry with the task, conditions, observation, interpretation if needed, next action, and next check. Keep individual observations private and separate from the shared class content and proposed homework.
